# Findings and next steps

Written on 2026-10-02, from the pipeline run of the same day (129 flux sites, 2,177 site-years).
This file is an interpretation and is not regenerated by the pipeline. The numbers behind it are in
[results_report.md](results_report.md); if the pipeline is rerun, check them against that report.

All effects are **within sites**: days of EOS shift per +1 within-site SD of the predictor (each year
minus its site's mean, standard errors clustered by site); negative = earlier senescence.
EOS90 / EOS50 / EOS10 = onset / middle / end of senescence.

## In one paragraph

More GPP before the summer solstice goes with an earlier **onset** of senescence (EOS90), by about
1-2.5 days per SD, in every EOS source. The result survives leaving any single site out, every
phenology QC threshold tried, and predictors that do not depend on leaf-out. Summed over the whole
season the effect of GPP on EOS90 is close to zero. That is the pattern "Zani and Lu are both right"
predicts. The opposite, positive effect of post-solstice GPP is small (+1 day), appears only in the
satellite indices and mostly disappears once spring temperature is held fixed, so the data show a
fading of the early effect and not a cancellation by a late one.
Nothing of this holds for the **end** of senescence (EOS10). Three things limit the claim: in
deciduous forests leaf-out date explains EOS90 and early GPP adds nothing; about half of the effect is
a spring-temperature effect (with temperature, radiation and water held fixed, GPP keeps about -1.2
days per SD, mostly at evergreen sites); and only the satellite samples are large enough to detect an
effect of this size.

## Update: more PhenoCam data and measured soil water

Added after the sections below were written. PhenoCam numbers in sections 1, 3 and 8 are from the
smaller PhenoCam sample; the current ones are in [results_report.md](results_report.md).

**PhenoCam**
- PhenoCam starts a new ROI file each time a camera's field of view shifts, and the pipeline used only
  one file per site. It now uses all of them, year by year (142 files instead of 58), and also accepts
  cameras 1-2 km away that look at the site's own vegetation type (3 more sites: 61 matched).
- Site-years passing QC: **266, up from 223**; sites with at least 3 years: 30, up from 25.
- **The PhenoCam pre-solstice effect is weaker on the larger sample.** Without the temperature
  covariate: -1.2 (sum from leaf-out) and -0.7 (calendar), against -2.0 and -1.3 before. With spring
  temperature held fixed: -0.7 / -0.2 / -0.5 (sum / calendar / rate), none significant. Spring
  temperature itself: -2.4 to -2.6 (p <= 0.01).
- The sign is still negative with every site left out, but PhenoCam now supports the temperature
  effect more clearly than the GPP effect. Power is still low (25-28%).
- The counts of the formal test did not change (EOS90: all three parts in 3 of 20 tests with the sum,
  0 with calendar windows or the rate).

**Soil water**
- Soil water content (shallowest sensor) is now extracted for 128 of 151 sites; 96 sites and 827
  site-years have it for the 90 days before the solstice. It is used as plant-available water: relative
  to each site's own dry and wet ends.
- **Soil water does not explain EOS90 and does not change the GPP effect.** At summer-green sites
  (1,274 site-years stacked): soil water +0.3 (n.s.); GPP -1.8 with or without it, -1.2 with temperature
  also fixed (p = 0.03). The same holds for soil water after the solstice.
- **Dry-summer sites:** only 6 have soil water (81 site-years stacked) and nothing is significant there.
  The climatic water balance still predicts EOS90 (+1.8, p < 0.01, 9 sites). So the water effect at
  dry-summer sites rests on rainfall minus evaporative demand, not yet on measured soil water.

## 3. How solid is the pre-solstice effect? (step 43)

- **Leave one site out.** The slope stays negative in 100% of the refits, for all four sources and both
  predictors. For NIRv it stays significant in every refit (-1.1 to -1.6); for satellite NDVI with the
  sum from leaf-out as well (-1.7 to -2.1). The most influential sites are US-NC2 (satellite), JP-Tmd
  (tower NDVI) and US-KFS (PhenoCam); without them the slope shrinks by 0.2-0.8 but keeps its sign.
- **QC thresholds.** Over 12 combinations of minimum fit R2 (0.7 / 0.8 / 0.9) and the site-median rule
  (30 / 60 / 90 days / off), 95 of 96 slopes are negative. Satellite NDVI ranges from -0.7 to -2.2 and
  NIRv from -0.6 to -1.9. PhenoCam and tower NDVI vary more (-0.5 to -3.2) because their samples are small.
- **Power.** To detect 1.5 days per SD with 80% power, about 380-510 site-years are needed. Satellite
  NDVI (395-456) and NIRv (650-681) have that; their power is 82-93%. PhenoCam (161-186 site-years) and
  tower NDVI (318-357) have 21-24% power and would need 850-1,900 site-years because their slopes are
  noisier. On the site-years PhenoCam shares with the satellite (66-111), power is 12-41%.
- **Consequence:** only the satellite indices can confirm or reject an effect of this size. PhenoCam
  and tower NDVI can only show a consistent sign, which they do.

## 4. Why? (step 44)

**Leaf-out date (deciduous forests)**

- Early GPP and leaf-out date are correlated within sites (r = -0.41 for the sum from leaf-out, -0.21
  for calendar windows).
- With all sources stacked (623 site-years, 29 sites), **leaf-out date predicts EOS90 (+1.3 to +1.4
  days per SD, p <= 0.02) and early GPP does not** (-0.2 to -0.4, p > 0.5) when both are in the model.
  An earlier leaf-out goes with an earlier onset of senescence.
- Satellite NDVI shows this most clearly: leaf-out +3.7 (p < 0.001), GPP +0.4. For NIRv neither is
  significant.
- **Over all vegetation types it is the reverse**: early GPP keeps its effect (-1.6 to -1.9, p < 0.001)
  and leaf-out date has none. The leaf-out explanation is specific to deciduous forests.

**Water (dry-summer sites)**

- Only 9 dry-summer sites have enough EOS90 years (131-135 site-years stacked).
- There, **the water balance of the 90 days before the solstice predicts EOS90 (+1.9 to +2.1 days per
  SD, p <= 0.01): a wet spring delays senescence. Pre-solstice GPP has no effect** (-0.5 to +0.4) with
  or without water in the model.
- The large dry-summer effect reported before (-3.5) rests on 42 NIRv site-years and is not supported
  by this larger test.
- At summer-green sites water balance has no effect and the GPP effect is unchanged by it (-1.7 to -1.8).
- There is no soil-moisture variable in the extracted FLUXNET table; the climatic water balance stands
  in for it.

**Sink variables in place of GPP**

- NPP, autotrophic, growth and maintenance respiration are GPP times a factor (within-site r with GPP
  0.2-0.8). **None explains EOS90 better than GPP, and none keeps an effect next to GPP.**
- **Spring temperature does as well as GPP or better.** The temperature part of the sink index (60 days
  before the solstice) gives -1.3 to -2.9 days per SD in all four sources (p <= 0.04), and keeps its
  effect next to GPP for NIRv (-1.8) and tower NDVI (-2.8). For NIRv both stay significant together.
- The water part of the index has no consistent effect.

**Spring temperature against GPP** (60 days before the solstice; all sources stacked, 1,680 site-years)

| Sites | GPP alone | T alone | GPP, T fixed | T, GPP fixed | GPP, all weather fixed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| All | -1.6* | -2.2* | -1.2* | -2.0* | -1.2* |
| Summer-green | -1.7* | -2.3* | -1.3* | -2.0* | -1.3* |
| Evergreen | -2.0* | -2.6* | -1.7* | -2.3* | -1.5* |
| Deciduous | -0.7 | -1.3* | -0.3 | -1.2* | -0.2 |

\* two-sided p < 0.05. "All weather" = temperature, radiation and water balance of the same window.

- **Both act, and temperature is the stronger one.** GPP and temperature are only weakly correlated
  within sites (r = 0.19), so they can be told apart. With both in the model, temperature gives -2.0 and
  GPP -1.2; temperature alone explains about three times more variance than GPP alone adds.
- **A GPP effect of about -1.2 days per SD remains after temperature, radiation and water are held
  fixed.** Per source it is significant only for NIRv (-1.2, p = 0.005); NDVI -0.8, tower NDVI -1.3 and
  PhenoCam -0.9 have the same sign.
- **It comes from evergreen sites** (-1.5 to -1.9). In deciduous forests GPP has no effect once
  temperature or leaf-out is in the model.
- **Years in which the two diverge do not decide it.** EOS90 is early when spring is warm and GPP high
  (-2.6 days, p < 0.001) and late when it is cool and GPP low (+1.7). In warm, low-GPP years it is
  unchanged (0.0) and in cool, high-GPP years slightly late (+1.1, n.s.). If GPP alone drove the effect,
  cool high-GPP years would be early; they are not. If temperature alone did, warm low-GPP years would
  be early; they are not either.
- **Reading:** the "pre-solstice GPP" effect is about half a spring-temperature effect. The remaining
  half is a GPP effect that the weather does not explain, and it is carried by evergreen conifers.

## 5. When in the season? (step 34)

The scan now starts 120 days before the solstice and is fitted within sites.

- For EOS90 the most negative 30-day GPP window starts **50-60 days before the solstice** (late April)
  for the three canopy-wide sources: NDVI -1.5 (p = 0.006), NIRv -1.7 (p < 0.001), tower NDVI -2.6
  (p = 0.03). PhenoCam: 30 days before, -1.3, not significant.
- The minimum is no longer on the edge of the scanned range. The effect sits in spring, earlier than
  the weeks around the solstice where Zohner et al. (2023) place it.
- For EOS10 there is no significant window in any source.

## 6. Climate vs carbon (step 35, EOS10, within sites)

- **Climate explains 4-11% of the year-to-year variance of EOS10.** Adding GPP, NPP and leaf-out date
  raises it by 1-4 points.
- **Out-of-sample skill is nil**: leave-one-site-out R2 is -0.03 to +0.09 for satellite and tower, and
  negative for PhenoCam. The mixed-model values reported before (0.16-0.27) came from between-site
  differences.
- Leaf-out date is negative in all four sources (-1.2 to -3.3) but significant only for tower NDVI.
- For NIRv, a warm spring advances EOS10 (-2.9) and a warm late summer delays it (+2.1).

## 7. Other within-site results (steps 37, 38)

- **Rate vs sum (EOS10, leaf-out to solstice).** The GPP rate is negative for NDVI (-1.1) and tower
  NDVI (-2.9); the sum is not. With rate and window length together, window length is positive
  (+1.8 to +2.8): an early leaf-out goes with a later EOS10.
- **By plant type:** of 780 tests, 90 (11.5%) have uncorrected p < 0.05 and one survives FDR correction
  (grass/shrub, satellite NDVI: post-solstice GPP rate, +5.1). No effect is robust across sources.

## 8. PhenoCam vs satellite (step 42)

- **They do not disagree.** On the shared site-years the pre-solstice effect is the same in both
  (PhenoCam -1.8 vs NDVI -1.7 on 81 site-years; PhenoCam -1.0 vs NIRv -1.0 on 111), and the difference
  model finds no effect of GPP on the gap between the two EOS measures.
- **Nothing is significant on the shared sample**; see the power calculation above.
- **Same stage, shifted in time.** Satellite NDVI runs 15-17 days behind PhenoCam at every level, and
  the best match is the same level (EOS50 with EOS50, r = 0.63). NIRv agrees poorly (r = 0.2-0.3).

## 9. EOS sources, CUE, drought (steps 30-32, 39)

- Sentinel-2 raised the clear images per site-year to about 32-51; the satellite EOS is as stable as
  PhenoCam (within-site SD 6.5-10.6 days vs 7.3-11.4).
- Annual CUE averages 0.52; 0.62 before and 0.59 after the solstice.
- Drought years show earlier senescence in satellite NDVI (EOS10 -4.9 days, EOS50 -4.0) and NIRv
  (EOS50 -2.3); the ground sources agree in sign only.

## What should be done next

**To strengthen the main result**
1. Decide how to word the claim. With spring temperature held fixed the early-GPP effect remains but
   the cancelling post-solstice effect does not; "Zani and Lu are both right" then rests on a weak early
   effect that is diluted over the season, not on two opposite effects.
2. More ground-based site-years. PhenoCam and tower NDVI have about a fifth of the power needed. Adding
   PhenoCam sites beyond the flux network would not help (no GPP); longer tower records would.
3. Deciduous forests: the result there is "leaf-out date, not GPP". Test whether leaf-out acts through
   leaf age (a fixed leaf life span) with the PhenoCam leaf-out dates, which are more precise.
4. Dry-summer sites: only 6 have both soil water and enough EOS90 years. Add the Mediterranean sites
   that currently fail the phenology QC before drawing a conclusion about water there.

**To test the sink mechanism directly**
5. Dendrometer growth at CH-Dav, CZ-BK1, CZ-Stn and CZ-RAJ (DenDrought2018), CH-Lae (TreeNet) and
   US-Ha1 (HF149): does growth before the solstice, or the date growth stops, relate to EOS90?
6. Save mR0 and T0 in step 26 so that maintenance respiration can be computed from temperature and
   biomass, independently of the same day's GPP. The respiration terms used now are GPP times a factor.

**Housekeeping**
7. Rebuild the environment on Python 3.11 or newer: Earth Engine stops supporting 3.10 on 2026-10-04.
8. Make step 26 save per site, so that an interrupted run does not cost an hour.
9. Report the two MATLAB issues to the authors of the CUE code.
